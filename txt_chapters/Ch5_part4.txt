Algorithm 3 integrates normal control, route updates, and corridor ownership under one simulation-
clock writer. Each iteration advances SUMO once, reads a complete observation set, creates a times-
tamped snapshot, and then performs routing and corridor updates at their configured intervals. For each
intersection, the corridor or the normal controller can request an action, but both pass through the same
safety filter and only the TraCI adapter applies the result. Applied actions, metric samples, and mis-
sion events are recorded before a dashboard view is published. This ordering prevents a slow client,
background video job, or concurrent controller from advancing simulation time or acting on a partially
updated state. A snapshot carries its effective step, production time, source, and validity interval so late
data is deliberately mapped or rejected. At termination, the manifest and checksums are finalized even
for stopped or failed runs. The loop therefore defines both concurrency ownership and the causal order
needed to replay an experimental decision.

used a reduced version of the protocol, the reduction is stated beside the rule.

5.8.3 Metric Definitions

Metric definitions are shared across controllers and versioned with the run. For N completed vehicles
with travel times Ti, mean travel time is T¯ = N−1 ∑i Ti. Queue length is sampled at fixed simulation inter-
vals and reported as both time mean and maximum by approach. Throughput counts vehicles crossing
declared network boundaries during the measurement interval, while the demand manifest and actual
departures prevent a low completion count from being misread. Fairness includes maximum red time
and the approach-level delay distribution because an aggregate mean can conceal starvation. Ambulance
travel time is the primary emergency measure. Excess delay is the observed travel time minus the free-
flow time of the same realized edge sequence, while route benefit is assessed separately by comparing
matched routing policies. This distinction prevents a longer selected path from receiving an artificial
advantage through an unrelated reference path. Ordinary-traffic impact covers non-emergency vehicles
during the mission and the declared recovery interval. Route computation time is measured separately
from simulated travel time, and revision count identifies unstable routing. Because a poor policy can
strand vehicles so that only short trips finish, a travel-time mean that also includes unfinished vehicles is

5.8.4 Reproducibility Package

Each run retains the source revision, dependency manifest, SUMO version, network and route check-
sums, controller and model version, full configuration, seed, logs, metric schema, and terminal reason.
Applied decisions are stored separately from controller requests, and route revisions identify the cost
snapshot and trigger that produced them. Large external video and pretrained weights may be refer-
CHAPTER 5. PROPOSED APPROACH AND METHODOLOGY  64

enced by a license-aware URI and checksum when redistribution is not permitted. The configuration
stored in a manifest is sufficient to repeat a run, and every manual NetEdit change must be committed
as a versioned network artifact rather than left in an operator’s local state. Derived plots and summary
tables retain their source run identifiers and analysis-script revision. Failed and cancelled runs keep their
partial logs and explicit terminal state. This package does not guarantee identical hardware timing, but it
makes scenario semantics, causal events, and analytical transformations inspectable enough for another
team member to reproduce the experiment or explain a difference.

5.9 Threats to Validity

Construct validity is threatened when a queue proxy, waiting-time implementation, or reward compo-
nent does not represent the concept named in the report. Definitions and units are therefore versioned,
and image-space proxies are not relabeled as physical quantities without calibration. Internal validity
can be weakened by unmatched demand, different warm-up, hidden seed changes, or controller-specific
failures; paired manifests, one measurement implementation, and retention of failed runs reduce these
risks. External validity remains limited by synthetic demand, map inaccuracies, SUMO car-following
assumptions, a small selected network, and domain shift between public video and Lahore traffic. Con-
clusions are bounded to the tested network, demand families, software versions, and video sources, with
no claim about clinical response time or public-road safety. Conclusion validity can be weakened by too
few repetitions, test-set tuning, selective failure removal, or reporting only means. Pilot variance, held-
out scenarios, paired comparisons, variability, and sensitivity analysis address those concerns. Paired
confidence intervals are reported together with effect sizes, and significance alone is not treated as prac-
tical value.

5.10 Conclusion

The methodology specifies a staged progression from validated measurements and baselines to inte-
grated emergency control. Network provenance and demand manifests support repeatable scenarios;
source-separated video evaluation prevents perception estimates from being confused with simulator
truth; and common safety constraints permit a fair controller comparison. Routing uses time-consistent
nonnegative costs, validity checks, and hysteresis, while the corridor lifecycle makes request, clearance,
active priority, passage, cancellation, and recovery observable. The comparison matrix separates per-
ception, normal control, routing, corridor effect, and robustness so the cause of a result can be explained.
Held-out scenarios, repeated seeds, explicit metrics, retained failures, and reproducibility artifacts pro-
tect the final evaluation from undocumented tuning. Most importantly, the procedure allows a learned

controller, tracker, rerouting policy, or corridor configuration to produce a negative or mixed finding
without prompting selective changes to the test protocol. The next chapter maps these methodological
responsibilities to components, interfaces, state ownership, and failure handling.
