Chapter 3 Literature Review / Related Work

This chapter examines the research needed to justify Manzil's design rather than treating the use of fa-
miliar tools as a contribution. The review is organized around five decisions: how traffic is observed,
how normal signals are controlled, how learned policies are evaluated, how emergency routes are se-
lected, and how signal priority is activated and recovered. Each study is considered in its original data or
simulation setting because reported values from incompatible benchmarks cannot be treated as a com-
mon comparison. The discussion identifies the method, evidence, limitations, and direct implication for
a Manzil design choice. Research papers are distinguished from official software documentation, which
is used to establish interface capabilities but is not counted toward the study requirement. The purpose
is not to assemble every publication on intelligent transportation. It is to establish whether the selected
components are technically defensible, which assumptions must be recorded, and where a controlled
undergraduate experiment can add useful evidence. The final synthesis consequently states a precise
integration question instead of claiming that the use of YOLO, reinforcement learning, or shortest-path
search is itself novel.

3.1 Definitions, Acronyms, and Abbreviations

Additional terms used specifically in the literature review are defined below.

IDF1: Identity F1 score, a tracking measure based on correctly identified detections across a sequence.
IoU: Intersection over Union, the overlap ratio commonly used when matching a predicted bounding
box with a reference box.
mAP: Mean Average Precision, a detection measure aggregated across classes or overlap thresholds
under a stated protocol.
MARL: Multi-agent reinforcement learning, in which several decision-making agents learn within a
shared environment.
MDP: Markov Decision Process, a formal model of states, actions, transitions, and rewards used to
define an RL problem.
OSM: OpenStreetMap, the collaborative geographic data source considered for preparing the SUMO
road network.

3.2 Detailed Literature Review

The approved proposal supplied the initial bibliography, after which each retained study was checked
against a publisher page, proceedings record, DOI, or official open-access paper. Selection required
direct relevance to vehicle detection and tracking, traffic-state estimation, signal control, simulation,
graph routing, or emergency preemption. The review also applies consistent terminology. Detection
refers to locating and classifying vehicles within one frame, while tracking refers to associating those
observations through time. A detector therefore cannot produce reliable passage counts by itself. Signal
priority describes preferential treatment that may remain within normal timing constraints, whereas
preemption may interrupt normal operation for an emergency request. Manzil uses the term moving
green corridor for a coordinated sequence of bounded interventions ahead of the ambulance, not for a
permanently green route. These distinctions affect the interpretation of prior results and the design of
the experiments. A paper reporting benchmark detection accuracy does not establish a calibrated queue
estimate, and a study of isolated preemption does not automatically validate network-level routing. The
following sections preserve those boundaries when relating the literature to Manzil.

3.2.1 Vision-Based Traffic Observation

This theme reviews how vehicles are detected in single frames, associated into tracks over time, and
evaluated on traffic datasets. It matters for Manzil because counts and queue indicators are only as reli-
able as the complete detection, tracking, and region-assignment chain. The review informed three FYP-I
choices: a YOLO-family detector fine-tuned rather than used unchanged, ByteTrack as the tracker, and
UA-DETRAC as the main evaluation source with a split by whole sequence. The subsections below
review one-stage detection, multi-object tracking, and the traffic datasets used for evaluation.

3.2.1.1 One-Stage Detection

Redmon et al. formulated YOLO as a single network that predicts bounding boxes and class probabil-
ities from an image in one evaluation [7]. The study established the feasibility of real-time one-stage
detection and reported a strong speed advantage, while also identifying localization error as a relative
weakness. The design is relevant to Manzil because a compact inference path is practical for recorded
traffic video and may later support a live demonstration on available hardware. The evidence does not
establish accuracy for every vehicle class, road layout, camera height, weather condition, or Lahore
traffic scene. A selected pretrained checkpoint must therefore be evaluated on the project's own held-
out sequences, and confidence or intersection-over-union thresholds cannot be copied from an unrelated
benchmark. Frame-level boxes also do not provide stable counts by themselves. The same vehicle may
CHAPTER 3. LITERATURE REVIEW / RELATED WORK  13

be detected in many frames, an occluded vehicle may disappear temporarily, and perspective changes
apparent size across the image. Manzil consequently treats one-stage detection as the first part of a
measurement pipeline that also requires tracking, road-region assignment, temporal aggregation, and
explicit uncertainty.

3.2.1.2 Multi-Object Tracking

Wojke et al. extended a simple online tracker with a learned appearance metric to improve associa-
tion through longer occlusions [8]. Appearance information can reduce identity switches, but it adds
a feature-extraction model, processing cost, and sensitivity to domain differences. ByteTrack follows
a different strategy by associating high-confidence detections first and then attempting to recover true
objects from lower-confidence boxes rather than discarding them [2]. The authors evaluated it across
several tracking benchmarks and reported strong accuracy with real-time throughput on their stated hard-
ware. This approach is relevant to traffic video because occlusion and motion blur often reduce detection
confidence. Neither benchmark establishes Manzil's processing speed, lane assignment, or queue accu-
racy. An identity-preserving track still requires a road-contact point, direction rule, and region geometry
before it becomes a valid traffic observation. These studies therefore motivate an empirical tracker
choice based on held-out count error, identity stability where identities are needed, failure under oc-
clusion, and end-to-end throughput on the team's hardware. If a simpler association method produces
equally reliable aggregate counts, its lower complexity may be preferable.

3.2.1.3 Traffic Datasets and Relevance to Manzil

BDD100K provides a large and diverse driving dataset with detection, tracking, and other driving-
scene annotations collected across varied locations, weather, and times [9]. Its diversity is useful for
model initialization and stress testing, although its primarily vehicle-mounted viewpoint differs from a
fixed roadside or elevated camera. UA-DETRAC focuses directly on vehicle detection and tracking in
traffic-surveillance video, making its camera geometry and evaluation protocol closer to an intersection-
observation task [4]. Its scenes still cannot be assumed to represent Lahore traffic composition, mo-
torcycle and rickshaw prevalence, lane discipline, lighting, or camera placement. The two datasets are
therefore complementary references rather than automatic substitutes for local validation. Manzil uses
licensed public sequences, mainly UA-DETRAC, for reproducible evaluation, and a small, separately
documented Lahore sample is planned for FYP-II. These datasets also influence the split strategy. Adja-
cent video frames are highly correlated, so a random frame split can place nearly identical observations
in training and test data. The project therefore splits by continuous sequence, retains a held-out evalua-
tion set, and does not tune thresholds on that set.

3.2.2 Conventional and Adaptive Signal Control

This theme covers the non-learning controllers that any learned policy must be compared against. A
credible fixed-time plan and a transparent traffic-responsive rule are needed both as baselines and as di-
agnostic references for the simulation and metrics. In FYP-I, Manzil implemented a fixed-time controller
with plans tuned per demand profile and a pressure-based adaptive controller derived from max-pressure
control, both constrained by the same safety filter. The subsections below review the role of fixed timing,
the max-pressure basis of the adaptive rule, and how both shape the comparison.

3.2.2.1 Fixed-Time Baseline

A fixed-time controller remains necessary even when it is not expected to dominate every scenario. It
provides a stable reference against which the value of traffic-responsive decisions can be measured,
and it helps reveal whether demand generation or metric collection is biased before more complex con-
trollers are introduced. The baseline must be credible rather than deliberately weak. Manzil therefore
documents its phase sequence, green times, yellow and all-red clearance, cycle length, offsets where
relevant, minimum service, and the demand conditions used to choose those settings. The same phase
definitions and safety transitions are shared with the adaptive and learned controllers so that a compari-
son does not reward one controller for using an illegal or more permissive movement set. Fixed timing is
also useful for route and corridor ablations because its behavior is deterministic and easy to reproduce.
If the fixed plan is severely under-saturated or over-saturated in every scenario, differences in controller
performance will be difficult to interpret. A capacity and queue check therefore confirms that the sce-
nario family contains meaningful light, medium, and heavy conditions instead of a single demand level
chosen to favor adaptation.

3.2.2.2 Max-Pressure Control

Varaiya formalized max-pressure control for a network of signalized intersections using differences be-
tween upstream and downstream queue conditions [10]. Selecting a phase that reduces network pressure
has a clearer transportation basis than combining arbitrary percentages without explaining what the score
represents. The approach is responsive and can operate from local movement information, which makes
it a valuable non-learning reference for Manzil. Its assumptions still matter. Queue observations must
correspond to valid movements, downstream storage must be represented, and a theoretical selection
rule does not remove practical minimum-green, yellow, all-red, or maximum-red constraints. Manzil's
rule-based adaptive controller therefore uses a pressure-inspired score computed from the upstream and
downstream queues of each phase. Waiting time and a maximum-red limit remain as explicit fairness
safeguards so that a low-volume approach is not postponed indefinitely. The controller also provides a
CHAPTER 3. LITERATURE REVIEW / RELATED WORK  15

reason for each request, allowing reviewers to see which queues and constraints influenced the decision.
This transparency makes the baseline useful even if the later reinforcement-learning policy does not
outperform it.

3.2.2.3 Relevance to Manzil

The conventional-control literature determines both the baseline design and the limits of any later com-
parison. Manzil implements a documented fixed plan first because it provides a stable reference for vali-
dating demand, signal transitions, and metric collection. A pressure-inspired controller then responds to
current movement conditions while using the same legal phases, yellow intervals, all-red clearance, and
minimum-service rules. This shared safety envelope prevents the adaptive method from gaining an arti-
ficial advantage through a more permissive phase program. The rule-based controller must also expose
the queues, downstream conditions, waiting time, and constraint that produced each requested action.
Such records make an unexpected result diagnosable and provide a meaningful comparison for a learned
policy. Evaluation covers light, medium, heavy, and uneven demand with matched seeds rather than one
scenario selected after observing the outcome. Network averages are accompanied by maximum queues
and maximum-red or fairness measures, since a lower mean delay can hide repeated postponement of a
lightly loaded approach. These decisions convert the reviewed theory into an auditable baseline rather
than an informal demonstration.

3.2.3 Reinforcement Learning and Simulation

This theme reviews reinforcement learning for traffic-signal control and the simulators used to train and
evaluate it. The literature shows that learned controllers depend heavily on state, reward, action design,
and training scenarios, and that multi-intersection learning adds coordination and non-stationarity prob-
lems. These findings led Manzil to train a single parameter-sharing Double DQN on a small grid in
SUMO, with the safety filter outside the learned policy and held-out seeds for evaluation. The subsec-
tions below cover value-based learning, pressure-informed and multi-agent extensions, and the choice
of simulator.

3.2.3.1 Deep RL for Signal Control

Liang et al. used deep reinforcement learning to adjust traffic-light cycle behavior from observed traffic
state [11]. The study demonstrates how an action definition, decision interval, state representation, and
reward can be joined into a trainable signal controller. It also illustrates a limitation common to traffic
RL: performance obtained for a particular intersection, simulator configuration, demand pattern, and
training budget cannot be assumed to transfer to a different network. Manzil therefore does not treat a
CHAPTER 3. LITERATURE REVIEW / RELATED WORK  16

reported improvement as an expected project target and did not select deep Q-learning merely because it
is familiar. A value-based method is feasible only after the discrete action space is bounded, legal phase
transitions are enforced outside the learned policy, and fixed and rule-based baselines behave correctly.
State normalization and reward components must be declared before evaluation, while training and
tuning scenarios must remain separate from held-out tests. The policy is assessed using traffic measures
and safety interventions, not training return alone. If a simpler controller performs as well under the
selected scenarios, that result is reported rather than interpreted as a failure to produce a sufficiently
complex model.

3.2.3.2 Pressure-Based and Multi-Agent Methods

PressLight connects reinforcement-learning state and reward design to the transportation concept of max
pressure [12]. The paper argues that a theoretically grounded pressure signal can reduce reliance on ar-
bitrary handcrafted objectives and evaluates the method against conventional and learning baselines in
arterial networks. This is relevant to Manzil because a reward based only on total waiting time may con-
ceal spillback, unbalanced approaches, or repeated postponement of a minor movement. Pressure can
provide a principal network-efficiency signal, while explicit penalties or constraints can address starva-
tion and unnecessary switching. The study does not remove the need for careful evaluation. Topology,
movement mapping, flows, phase definitions, observation range, and training budget all influence the
learned result. Manzil must therefore record the exact state features, normalization, action timing, re-
ward components, and safety interventions used by the selected policy. Emergency priority remains a
supervisory state machine rather than a rare reward event folded into normal training. That separation
permits the normal-controller comparison to be interpreted independently and lets the additional effect
of the corridor be measured through a controlled ablation.

FRAP represents competition among signal phases and exploits structural relationships so that equiva-
lent movements need not be learned as unrelated cases [13]. This is useful where an intersection contains
several competing phases, but it requires an accurate phase graph and a more specialized representation.
CoLight addresses cooperation across intersections through graph attention, allowing neighboring con-
ditions to influence an agent's decision [14]. Chu et al. similarly study decentralized multi-agent deep
reinforcement learning at larger scale and emphasize the importance of communication and partial ob-
servability [15]. Together, these studies show why the phrase "multi-intersection RL" is not a complete
method. A project must state whether one centralized policy chooses all actions, independent agents op-
erate without communication, or cooperating agents exchange defined observations. Multi-agent learn-
ing also introduces non-stationarity because each agent experiences an environment affected by policies
that are changing at the same time. For Manzil, attention-based coordination may exceed the available
CHAPTER 3. LITERATURE REVIEW / RELATED WORK  17

tuning budget and make attribution difficult. The design therefore uses a small network and one shared
policy, adopting useful phase-structure ideas without claiming to reproduce every cited architecture.

3.2.3.3 Simulator Choice

CityFlow was introduced as a reinforcement-learning environment designed for large-scale city traffic
and high simulation throughput [16]. It is an important alternative when training speed and very large
networks dominate the design. SUMO is retained for Manzil because the approved proposal already de-
pends on its microscopic vehicle model, traffic-light programs, OpenStreetMap conversion tools, NetE-
dit workflow, and TraCI interface. Lopez et al. describe the simulator's architecture and its support
for detailed, externally controlled experiments [17]. This combination is appropriate for a manageable
multi-intersection prototype in which route validity, individual ambulance movement, signal transitions,
and per-vehicle outcomes must be inspected. Retaining SUMO also avoids the additional work of show-
ing that a migrated network and demand model behave equivalently in another simulator. The choice
does not make simulation results equivalent to field evidence. Car-following parameters, lane connec-
tions, signal programs, vehicle insertion, and map cleanup can materially change outcomes. Manzil
therefore versions the network, demand, routes, simulator settings, and software release, and it limits
conclusions to the declared simulation conditions.

3.2.4 Dynamic Routing and Emergency Priority

This theme covers shortest-path search on time-based costs and the research on emergency-vehicle rout-
ing and signal preemption. It supports two connected Manzil components: the routing service that
selects and revises the ambulance path, and the corridor coordinator that grants temporary, bounded
priority at upcoming junctions. FYP-I implemented both, using Dijkstra as the reference, A* with an
admissible heuristic, and a corridor lifecycle with safe transition, passage, and recovery. The subsections
below review the algorithms, the preemption literature, and the combined implications for Manzil.

3.2.4.1 Shortest-Path Algorithms

Dijkstra's algorithm finds a minimum-cost path on a graph whose edge weights are nonnegative [18].
Hart, Nilsson, and Raphael established the formal basis of A*, which adds a heuristic lower-bound
estimate to guide the search [1]. Both methods are well established, so selecting either algorithm is
not a research contribution by itself. Manzil's project-specific task is to build a graph that respects
valid SUMO vehicle movements and to define a cost that reflects time rather than distance alone. The
cost used by Manzil combines link travel time with non-overlapping estimates of queue discharge and
residual signal delay, all expressed in seconds. If measured link travel time already includes queue delay,
CHAPTER 3. LITERATURE REVIEW / RELATED WORK  18

a separate congestion term cannot be added without double-counting. Dijkstra provides the reference
route, while A* is permitted only when free-flow travel time forms an admissible lower bound under
nonnegative added delay. Dynamic rerouting also needs stability. A route changes when the current path
becomes infeasible or when an alternative offers a persistent improvement above a defined threshold.
Route revisions, no-route cases, and computation time are evaluated rather than treated as incidental log
messages.

3.2.4.2 Emergency Preemption

Humagain et al. systematically reviewed emergency-vehicle route optimization and preemption methods
and identified recurring concerns involving dynamic traffic data, travel-time objectives, ordinary-traffic
effects, safety, and limited real-world application [19]. The review supports an integrated evaluation
but does not justify the unsupported claim that integrated solutions do not exist. Qin and Khan exam-
ine transitions into emergency preemption and recovery to normal timing, including clearance and the
queues created by interruption [20]. Their work shows that preemption is a lifecycle rather than a single
instruction to set an approach green. Louati et al. investigate queue-aware emergency crossing across
signalized intersections [21]. This is relevant because a green signal does not guarantee an unobstructed
approach when the standing queue cannot discharge before the emergency vehicle arrives. Manzil there-
fore models requested, safe-transition, active-priority, passage, recovery, and cancellation states. It also
uses look-ahead activation, timeout, and bounded priority duration. The precise timing values remain
configurable simulation policies, not certified real-world safety parameters, and the evaluation reports
ordinary-traffic disruption together with ambulance benefit.

3.2.4.3 Relevance to Manzil

The literature establishes reliable building blocks while exposing the risks that arise when they are con-
nected. One-stage detection is fast enough to investigate traffic video, but useful traffic indicators require
tracking, road geometry, temporal rules, and validation on the intended viewpoint. Max-pressure and
reinforcement-learning research provide responsive control strategies, yet their comparison depends on
state representation, phase constraints, reward design, training data, and simulator configuration. Di-
jkstra and A* solve well-defined path problems, but emergency routing remains only as credible as
its movement graph, time-based costs, observation freshness, and rerouting stability. Preemption re-
search makes safe transition and recovery central, and the review of emergency methods shows why
ordinary-traffic disruption cannot be omitted. Manzil's contribution is consequently not a new detector,
shortest-path algorithm, or reinforcement-learning family. It is the controlled evaluation of an integrated
and traceable prototype in which perception and simulation evidence remain distinct, normal controllers

receive matched scenarios, routing and corridor effects are separated, and ambulance benefit is reported
with network disruption and recovery. The unresolved question is whether that integration yields con-
sistent benefits under the selected scenarios and constraints.

3.4 Conclusion

The review supports a staged technical strategy. Fixed-time and pressure-inspired controllers must op-
erate correctly before comparison with a learned controller, and measurement definitions must be fixed
before an optimization result is interpreted. Video experiments should assess the complete observation
chain rather than quote detector accuracy as a traffic-management result. Simulation experiments should
use matched seeds, identical safety constraints, and held-out scenarios so controller differences are not
confused with different demand. Emergency evaluation must separate route selection from signal prior-
ity because combining both changes would obscure the source of any improvement. The cited literature
justifies the selected building blocks but does not establish their performance for Manzil's network,
cameras, or computing resources. It also requires results to remain bounded to the tested viewpoints,
traffic demand, simulator settings, and route conditions. Chapter 4 converts these findings into traceable
requirements, while Chapter 5 defines the measurements and controlled comparisons needed to address
the evaluation objectives without claiming field deployment or certified safety evidence. The FYP-I im-
plementation followed this strategy, and Chapter 9 reports where its results agree or disagree with the
expectations drawn from the literature.
