Chapter 1 Introduction

This chapter introduces the Deliverable III report for Manzil, an intelligent traffic and emergency-routing
prototype developed as a FAST­NUCES Final Year Project. It states what the report is for, who is
expected to read it, and the terms used throughout the later chapters. It also separates work that has been
implemented and measured during FYP-I from work that remains planned for FYP-II, and the report
keeps that boundary clear.

1.1 Purpose of this Document

This Deliverable III report records the implemented FYP-I state of the Manzil prototype and the ev-
idence produced by it. It presents the requirements, method, and design of the system together with
its implementation and measured results. During FYP-I the team built a SUMO-based simulation core
with a single simulation adapter and safety filter, fixed-time, pressure-based adaptive, and parameter-
sharing Double DQN signal controllers, time-based ambulance routing with closure-triggered rerouting,
a moving green corridor, a YOLO-based vehicle detection, tracking, and counting pipeline trained on
Kaggle, and a first web dashboard. All controller, routing, and corridor results come from repeated,
seeded SUMO runs on a synthetic 3×3 signalized grid, and all perception results come from public
UA-DETRAC and COCO data. The report therefore claims measured simulation and video evidence,
not field performance or deployment readiness. Any performance statement in this report is tied to a
recorded configuration, matched baseline, stated metric, and repeatable set of runs.

1.2 Intended Audience

The primary audience is the FAST­NUCES FYP supervisor and evaluation panel. They require enough
detail to judge the relevance of the evaluation objectives, the implemented software, and the validity of
the experiments. The report is also an engineering reference for the student implementation team. Team
members should be able to derive component responsibilities, interface fields, scenario configurations,
fallback behavior, and verification from the chapters that follow. A secondary audience consists of
prospective traffic-management and emergency-service stakeholders who may wish to understand the
problem, operational assumptions, and limits of the prototype. These stakeholders are not represented
as current clients, project partners, or approving authorities. The writing therefore explains domain
terms when they first become important, but it retains the precision needed for implementation and
academic review. Business-level implications are kept separate from technical evidence, and proposed
functions are not described as operational capabilities. This distinction helps each audience find relevant

1.3 Definitions, Acronyms, and Abbreviations

Key terms and abbreviations used throughout the report are defined below.

A*: A shortest-path search using accumulated cost and an admissible lower-bound heuristic [1].
API: Application Programming Interface.
ByteTrack: A multi-object tracker that associates high-confidence detections first and then recovers
objects from lower-confidence detections [2].
CV: Computer vision used to derive observable traffic indicators from video.
DQN: Deep Q-Network, a value-based reinforcement-learning method that approximates action values
with a neural network.
Double DQN: A Deep Q-Network variant that selects the next action with the online network and eval-
uates it with a target network to reduce overestimation [3].
FYP-I / FYP-II: The first and second semesters of the Final Year Project; this report closes FYP-I.
Green corridor: A moving sequence of bounded signal-priority interventions ahead of an ambulance.
MAE: Mean absolute error, the average absolute difference between estimated and true values.
mAP50: Mean average precision at an intersection-over-union threshold of 0.5, a standard detection
accuracy measure.
MOT: Multi-object tracking, which associates detections into identities across video frames.
Queue proxy: An estimate of tracked slow or stationary vehicles in a defined lane region.
RL: Reinforcement learning, in which a policy is optimized using states, actions, and cumulative reward.
Signal preemption: A controlled signal change for an emergency request, including transition and re-
covery.
SUMO: Simulation of Urban MObility, a microscopic road-traffic simulator.
Throughput: Vehicles leaving a measured movement or network region per unit time.
TraCI: Traffic Control Interface for reading and changing a running SUMO simulation; libsumo offers
the same interface inside the Python process.
Traffic-state contract: A versioned observation structure with source, time, units, confidence, and valid-
ity.
UA-DETRAC: A public benchmark for detecting and tracking vehicles in video from fixed traffic cam-
eras [4].
Waiting time: Time accumulated below the configured SUMO speed threshold.
WAPE: Weighted absolute percentage error, the sum of absolute count errors divided by the sum of true

1.4 Conclusion

This chapter has established Manzil as a reproducible investigation of interacting traffic decisions rather
than a collection of unrelated artificial-intelligence features. The system studies how observable traffic
conditions can inform signal control, how dynamic routing can respond to network delay, and how an
ambulance can receive temporary priority without ignoring ordinary traffic. It has also fixed the evidence
boundary for this deliverable. The repository now contains implemented controllers, a trained detector
and DQN policy, a validated synthetic SUMO network, and recorded experimental results, so these are
reported as measured evidence. The Lahore road network, the full five-screen dashboard with a database,
filmed local video, and the full ten-seed protocol remain FYP-II work and are described as planned.
The defined terminology prevents video-derived proxies from being confused with exact simulator state
and distinguishes a moving corridor from an unsafe instruction to turn every signal green. A choice
is described as implemented only where its code, configuration, test evidence, or experimental record
exists in the project repository.

The rest of the report is organized as follows. Chapter 2 presents the project vision, objectives, and scope.
Chapter 3 reviews the related research on traffic perception, signal control, reinforcement learning, and
emergency routing. Chapter 4 specifies the functional and non-functional requirements. Chapter 5
describes the approach and the experimental protocol. Chapter 6 gives the high-level and low-level
design. Chapter 7 describes the implementation and its test cases. Chapter 8 is the user manual of the
prototype. Chapter 9 reports and discusses the experimental results. Chapter 10 concludes FYP-I and
gives the plan for FYP-II.
