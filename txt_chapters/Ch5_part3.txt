5.5 Dynamic Graph Routing

The routing graph represents legal vehicle movements rather than only geometric road adjacency. Nodes
may be junctions or lane and edge transition points, while edges retain length, allowed vehicle class,
free-flow time, current travel-time estimate, queue-discharge estimate, residual signal delay, closure
state, and observation time. The exact representation must reproduce SUMO route validity, including
turn restrictions and internal connections, so that an algorithm cannot return a visually plausible but
undrivable path. Every cost field states its unit and source, and stale observations are rejected or replaced
by a declared fallback estimate. Equation 5.4 expresses the primary cost in seconds. Link travel time,
signal delay, and queue delay are separated only when their estimators do not overlap; if a measured link
time already contains queuing, another queue penalty would double-count congestion. A hard closure
receives infinite cost, while temporary slowdowns retain finite nonnegative estimates. The complete cost
snapshot is versioned with any accepted route so that a later evaluator can reconstruct why it changed.

The primary edge cost is expressed in seconds:

ce(t) = Tˆerun(t) + Dˆ esignal(t) + Dˆ qeueue(t) + Me(t),  (5.4)

where Tˆerun excludes queue and signal delay, and Me(t) = 0 for an open edge and infinity for a hard
closure. When a measured total link travel time is available, it replaces the first three terms rather than
being added to them. Every cost snapshot records the estimator type, source, and freshness.

Dijkstra’s algorithm provides the reference minimum-cost path under nonnegative weights and requires
no heuristic assumption [18]. It therefore supplies a clear correctness baseline for route validity and
cost even when the selected network is small enough that search speed is not a practical limitation. A*
is permitted when its heuristic h(n) is a lower bound formed from straight-line distance divided by the

Algorithm 1 defines route selection and revision as one auditable transaction. It first rejects stale or
negative costs, applies hard closures, and searches from the ambulance’s valid current position. Dijk-

admissible free-flow lower bound. A missing path produces a recorded NO_ROUTE outcome and can-
cellation of future corridor requests rather than a partial route. When both current and candidate paths
are feasible, their remaining costs are evaluated under the same snapshot. The improvement threshold
δ , persistence window τ, and cooldown are declared before held-out tests, preventing small fluctuations
from causing oscillation. An infeasible current path bypasses the benefit threshold, but the replace-
ment must still pass continuity and vehicle-class checks. Acceptance increments the route version and

In the implementation, graph nodes are SUMO edges and arcs follow SUMO lane connections, so every

5.6 Moving Green Corridor

The moving corridor operates on a bounded number of intersections ahead of the simulated ambulance
instead of setting an entire route green. Activation considers estimated arrival, standing queue clearance,
and signal state rather than distance alone. Each request identifies the mission, immutable route version,
approach movement, predicted arrival, expiry, and priority level. Before serving that movement, the
coordinator completes the active phase’s minimum service and applies the configured yellow and all-red
clearance to conflicting movements. Passage is confirmed from the ambulance’s simulated edge and lane
transition, with a timeout detecting a missing or stalled vehicle. The intersection then enters recovery
and either completes a bounded compensation plan or returns control at a safe phase boundary. A revised
route cancels obsolete future requests before new look-ahead activation, preventing two route versions
from competing for a signal. These choices follow the transition and recovery emphasis in emergency-
preemption research [20] while keeping all timing values as simulation policies rather than certified field
parameters.

Algorithm 2 makes the intersection lifecycle explicit. A stale, expired, invalid, or obsolete request is
rejected before signal ownership can change. Valid requests wait for their activation condition, com-

priority duration. Confirmed passage, mission cancellation, route revision, or timeout all lead through
recovery rather than jumping to an arbitrary normal phase. The core scope permits one active ambulance,
avoiding an untested arbitration policy for competing emergencies; multiple emergency vehicles remain
future work. Ordinary-traffic harm is bounded through maximum priority duration and maximum-red
monitoring, and the evaluation records safety-filter interventions and recovery time. Exact limits are
reported as simulator settings, not public-road safety values, and the measured effect of the 5 s corridor
minimum is reported in Chapter 9. This lifecycle allows emergency benefit and network disruption to be
examined together while ensuring that cancellation is as carefully specified as successful passage. The
prototype uses a look-ahead of two junctions and holds priority for at most 60 s. A corridor request may
