5.4 Normal Traffic Controllers

Normal control decides which legal phase each signalized junction serves when no ambulance is present.
Manzil compares three controller families of increasing complexity under identical phases, timing con-
straints, and demand: a fixed-time plan, a transparent pressure-based rule, and a learned policy. All three
only request a phase; a shared safety filter applies minimum green, yellow, all-red, and a maximum-red
guard before any change reaches SUMO. The subsections below define each controller.

5.4.1 Fixed-Time Controller

The fixed controller uses a declared cycle and phase sequence appropriate to each selected junction.
Green, yellow, all-red, cycle, and offset values are versioned with the scenario, and the same movement
definitions and clearance rules constrain every competing controller. The baseline is not intentionally
starved or assigned unrealistic timing to make adaptation appear superior. Green splits are selected from
the declared demand and checked against approximate approach service so that at least the light scenario
is feasible and heavier scenarios create interpretable congestion rather than immediate gridlock. Its
deterministic schedule makes it useful for validating demand generation, metric collection, ambulance
route ablations, and replay before learning is introduced. If a run violates phase invariants or produces
implausible counts under fixed control, adaptive and reinforcement-learning comparisons are postponed
until the cause is resolved. The baseline therefore serves as both a comparator and a diagnostic reference,
not as a weak opponent constructed for a favorable result. In the implementation the fixed plans were
tuned separately for each demand profile on development seeds only, and a second fixed-time arm uses
one 35/35 s plan for all profiles to represent a controller that does not know the demand in advance.

where ql and qm are upstream and downstream queues for a served movement, wl is the maximum

Equation 5.2 assigns a transparent score to each legal candidate phase using upstream demand, down-
stream congestion, and the longest accumulated approach wait. The downstream term discourages serv-
ing a movement whose receiving link lacks space, while the wait term provides a direct safeguard against
repeatedly postponing a low-volume approach. Coefficients are configuration values established on de-
velopment scenarios and retained with the run manifest. At each decision instant, the current green is
held until minimum green has elapsed. It may then be extended when it remains the preferred phase and
maximum green has not been reached. A phase whose maximum-red limit is approached receives ex-
plicit promotion, and every switch inserts the same yellow and all-red sequence used by other controllers.
The controller returns a requested phase and its contributing values; it does not actuate SUMO directly.
This explanation record permits a reviewer to distinguish an algorithmic request from a safety-filter
transformation and to diagnose whether observed performance came from pressure, fairness promotion,

5.4.3 Reinforcement-Learning Formulation

The reinforcement-learning design is a parameter-sharing, discrete-action Deep Q-Network for the se-
lected small network, introduced only after the fixed and rule-based baselines are stable. Each controlled
intersection applies the same policy to a fixed-length local observation and requests either one legal
phase or a bounded extension. Parameter sharing limits the number of independently trained models
and permits consistent logging, while intersection identifiers and movement masks prevent an invalid
phase from entering the available action set. The safety layer retains authority over minimum green,
yellow and all-red clearance, and maximum-red constraints. Model architecture, observation normal-
ization, replay settings, exploration schedule, optimizer, training budget, and checkpoint-selection rule
were fixed before held-out evaluation. This choice is an initial experimental design rather than a claim
that Deep Q-learning is the best controller. A policy that is unstable, violates fairness limits, or gives no
consistent held-out benefit is reported as such rather than tuned on the evaluation seeds.

The state for each decision interval describes incoming and outgoing queue conditions, approach waiting

time, the current phase, elapsed phase time, and valid-action masks. Emergency status is excluded from

this normal-control policy because the corridor is evaluated as a separate supervisory intervention. All

queue and waiting inputs use common clipping and normalization ranges fixed during tuning. An action

requests a legal phase or a bounded extension. The safety layer remains authoritative. A candidate

reward is

where Pt = ∑m|qimn(t) − qomut(t)| is the nonnegative magnitude of movement pressure, Wt is normalized
waiting, Xt is a starvation violation, and Ct is an unnecessary-switch penalty. Coefficients and normal-
ization are fixed on training and tuning scenarios. Emergency performance is evaluated separately so
that the normal-controller comparison is not confounded by rare mission rewards.

Equation 5.3 combines network pressure, normalized waiting, starvation violations, and unnecessary
switching, with every component logged separately so that one favorable aggregate cannot conceal a
harmful term. Coefficients and normalization are chosen on training and tuning scenarios, then frozen.
Training records episode seed, demand profile, exploration schedule, optimizer settings, network check-
sum, reward components, software version, and candidate checkpoints. Checkpoint selection follows a
declared tuning criterion rather than the best result on the final scenarios. Evaluation uses deterministic
