Chapter 9 Experimental Results and Discussion

This chapter reports the experiments conducted during FYP-I and discusses what they show. It follows
the two evidence paths of Chapter 5. The perception experiments measure detection, counting, and
queue estimation on public UA-DETRAC and COCO data. The simulation experiments compare the
three signal controllers, static and dynamic ambulance routing, and the green corridor on the synthetic
SUMO grid. Every number in this chapter comes from a recorded run whose configuration, seed, and
software versions are stored in the project repository, and unfavourable results are reported alongside
favourable ones.

9.1 Experimental Setup

All simulation experiments use the 3×3 grid and the demand profiles of Chapter 7, with the timing of the
safety filter shared by every arm: 10 s minimum green, 3 s yellow, 2 s all-red, and a 90 s maximum red.
Controller comparisons use a 900 s warm-up followed by a 3,600 s measurement interval. Emergency
experiments dispatch one ambulance at 1,200 s from road left0A0 in the south-west to road C2top2 at
the north-east exit, a trip of about 79 s at free flow. The ambulance obeys red lights in every arm. Each
comparison is repeated on evaluation seeds 0 to 4, which were never used for tuning the fixed-time plans,
the adaptive parameters, or the DQN. Results are reported as the mean over the five seeds, the paired
difference against the reference arm with a 95 percent confidence interval, and the number of seeds on
which the arm was better. Five seeds are fewer than the ten of the full protocol, so the intervals are wide
where variation between seeds is large. Table 9.1 lists the experiments.

The error is almost entirely an over-count: no clip is under-counted by more than one vehicle. The
likely causes are false detections, such as the stationary “bus” on the kerb island in Figure 9.3 (left),
and identity switches near the line in dense traffic, which make one vehicle cross twice under two
identities. This explanation comes from inspecting samples, not from a frame-by-frame audit. The 39
motorcycle counts cannot be judged with UA-DETRAC, which does not label two-wheelers; some may
be real scooters or bicycles and some false detections. The queue proxy, the number of stopped tracked
vehicles in view, has a mean per-frame absolute error of 0.80 vehicles. On the busiest clip, with about

seed. The same reduction holds when unfinished vehicles are included (8.8 to 10.9 percent), so it is not
caused by vehicles being stranded. Throughput was unchanged within 0.1 percent, except for a gain
of 0.6 percent on heavy demand. The DQN also beat the adaptive controller on every travel-time and

62–76 s on average, with a worst case of 89 s, just below the 90 s guard. The guard itself intervened 5
times in the 20 DQN runs.

The adaptive controller reduced waiting time on every profile, by 1.9 to 21.1 percent, and queues by 2
to 22 percent, but it reduced travel time only on medium demand (−4.3 percent). On light, heavy, and
uneven demand travel time rose slightly, by up to 2.6 percent. The likely reason is more switching: on
heavy demand it changed phase 1,535 times against 648 for fixed-time control, and each change costs
5 s of clearance. This explanation fits the data but was not tested separately. When the demand is not
known in advance, represented by the single 35/35 s plan, the adaptive controller is clearly better on
uneven demand, with a travel time of 121.6 s against 149.3 s and a waiting time of 34.9 s against 59.4 s.
