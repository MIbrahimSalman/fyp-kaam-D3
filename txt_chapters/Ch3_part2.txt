3.2.3 Reinforcement Learning and Simulation

This theme reviews reinforcement learning for traffic-signal control and the simulators used to train and
evaluate it. The literature shows that learned controllers depend heavily on state, reward, action design,
and training scenarios, and that multi-intersection learning adds coordination and non-stationarity prob-
lems. These findings led Manzil to train a single parameter-sharing Double DQN on a small grid in
SUMO, with the safety filter outside the learned policy and held-out seeds for evaluation. The subsec-
tions below cover value-based learning, pressure-informed and multi-agent extensions, and the choice
of simulator.

3.2.3.1 Deep RL for Signal Control

Liang et al. used deep reinforcement learning to adjust traffic-light cycle behavior from observed traffic
state [11]. The study demonstrates how an action definition, decision interval, state representation, and
reward can be joined into a trainable signal controller. It also illustrates a limitation common to traffic
RL: performance obtained for a particular intersection, simulator configuration, demand pattern, and
training budget cannot be assumed to transfer to a different network. Manzil therefore does not treat a

reported improvement as an expected project target and did not select deep Q-learning merely because it
is familiar. A value-based method is feasible only after the discrete action space is bounded, legal phase
transitions are enforced outside the learned policy, and fixed and rule-based baselines behave correctly.
State normalization and reward components must be declared before evaluation, while training and
tuning scenarios must remain separate from held-out tests. The policy is assessed using traffic measures
and safety interventions, not training return alone. If a simpler controller performs as well under the
selected scenarios, that result is reported rather than interpreted as a failure to produce a sufficiently
complex model.

3.2.3.2 Pressure-Based and Multi-Agent Methods

PressLight connects reinforcement-learning state and reward design to the transportation concept of max
pressure [12]. The paper argues that a theoretically grounded pressure signal can reduce reliance on ar-
bitrary handcrafted objectives and evaluates the method against conventional and learning baselines in
arterial networks. This is relevant to Manzil because a reward based only on total waiting time may con-
ceal spillback, unbalanced approaches, or repeated postponement of a minor movement. Pressure can
provide a principal network-efficiency signal, while explicit penalties or constraints can address starva-
tion and unnecessary switching. The study does not remove the need for careful evaluation. Topology,
movement mapping, flows, phase definitions, observation range, and training budget all influence the
learned result. Manzil must therefore record the exact state features, normalization, action timing, re-
ward components, and safety interventions used by the selected policy. Emergency priority remains a
supervisory state machine rather than a rare reward event folded into normal training. That separation
permits the normal-controller comparison to be interpreted independently and lets the additional effect
of the corridor be measured through a controlled ablation.

FRAP represents competition among signal phases and exploits structural relationships so that equiva-
lent movements need not be learned as unrelated cases [13]. This is useful where an intersection contains
several competing phases, but it requires an accurate phase graph and a more specialized representation.
CoLight addresses cooperation across intersections through graph attention, allowing neighboring con-
ditions to influence an agent’s decision [14]. Chu et al. similarly study decentralized multi-agent deep
reinforcement learning at larger scale and emphasize the importance of communication and partial ob-
servability [15]. Together, these studies show why the phrase “multi-intersection RL” is not a complete
method. A project must state whether one centralized policy chooses all actions, independent agents op-
erate without communication, or cooperating agents exchange defined observations. Multi-agent learn-
ing also introduces non-stationarity because each agent experiences an environment affected by policies
that are changing at the same time. For Manzil, attention-based coordination may exceed the available

policy, adopting useful phase-structure ideas without claiming to reproduce every cited architecture.

3.2.3.3 Simulator Choice

CityFlow was introduced as a reinforcement-learning environment designed for large-scale city traffic
and high simulation throughput [16]. It is an important alternative when training speed and very large
networks dominate the design. SUMO is retained for Manzil because the approved proposal already de-
pends on its microscopic vehicle model, traffic-light programs, OpenStreetMap conversion tools, NetE-
dit workflow, and TraCI interface. Lopez et al. describe the simulator’s architecture and its support
for detailed, externally controlled experiments [17]. This combination is appropriate for a manageable
multi-intersection prototype in which route validity, individual ambulance movement, signal transitions,
and per-vehicle outcomes must be inspected. Retaining SUMO also avoids the additional work of show-
ing that a migrated network and demand model behave equivalently in another simulator. The choice
does not make simulation results equivalent to field evidence. Car-following parameters, lane connec-
tions, signal programs, vehicle insertion, and map cleanup can materially change outcomes. Manzil
therefore versions the network, demand, routes, simulator settings, and software release, and it limits
conclusions to the declared simulation conditions.

3.2.4 Dynamic Routing and Emergency Priority

This theme covers shortest-path search on time-based costs and the research on emergency-vehicle rout-
ing and signal preemption. It supports two connected Manzil components: the routing service that
selects and revises the ambulance path, and the corridor coordinator that grants temporary, bounded
priority at upcoming junctions. FYP-I implemented both, using Dijkstra as the reference, A* with an
admissible heuristic, and a corridor lifecycle with safe transition, passage, and recovery. The subsections
below review the algorithms, the preemption literature, and the combined implications for Manzil.

3.2.4.1 Shortest-Path Algorithms

Dijkstra’s algorithm finds a minimum-cost path on a graph whose edge weights are nonnegative [18].
Hart, Nilsson, and Raphael established the formal basis of A*, which adds a heuristic lower-bound
estimate to guide the search [1]. Both methods are well established, so selecting either algorithm is
not a research contribution by itself. Manzil’s project-specific task is to build a graph that respects
valid SUMO vehicle movements and to define a cost that reflects time rather than distance alone. The
cost used by Manzil combines link travel time with non-overlapping estimates of queue discharge and
residual signal delay, all expressed in seconds. If measured link travel time already includes queue delay,
CHAPTER 3. LITERATURE REVIEW / RELATED WORK  18

a separate congestion term cannot be added without double-counting. Dijkstra provides the reference
route, while A* is permitted only when free-flow travel time forms an admissible lower bound under
nonnegative added delay. Dynamic rerouting also needs stability. A route changes when the current path
becomes infeasible or when an alternative offers a persistent improvement above a defined threshold.
Route revisions, no-route cases, and computation time are evaluated rather than treated as incidental log
messages.

3.2.4.2 Emergency Preemption

Humagain et al. systematically reviewed emergency-vehicle route optimization and preemption methods
and identified recurring concerns involving dynamic traffic data, travel-time objectives, ordinary-traffic
effects, safety, and limited real-world application [19]. The review supports an integrated evaluation
but does not justify the unsupported claim that integrated solutions do not exist. Qin and Khan exam-
ine transitions into emergency preemption and recovery to normal timing, including clearance and the
queues created by interruption [20]. Their work shows that preemption is a lifecycle rather than a single
instruction to set an approach green. Louati et al. investigate queue-aware emergency crossing across
signalized intersections [21]. This is relevant because a green signal does not guarantee an unobstructed
approach when the standing queue cannot discharge before the emergency vehicle arrives. Manzil there-
fore models requested, safe-transition, active-priority, passage, recovery, and cancellation states. It also
uses look-ahead activation, timeout, and bounded priority duration. The precise timing values remain
configurable simulation policies, not certified real-world safety parameters, and the evaluation reports
ordinary-traffic disruption together with ambulance benefit.

3.2.4.3 Relevance to Manzil

The literature establishes reliable building blocks while exposing the risks that arise when they are con-
nected. One-stage detection is fast enough to investigate traffic video, but useful traffic indicators require
tracking, road geometry, temporal rules, and validation on the intended viewpoint. Max-pressure and
reinforcement-learning research provide responsive control strategies, yet their comparison depends on
state representation, phase constraints, reward design, training data, and simulator configuration. Di-
jkstra and A* solve well-defined path problems, but emergency routing remains only as credible as
its movement graph, time-based costs, observation freshness, and rerouting stability. Preemption re-
search makes safe transition and recovery central, and the review of emergency methods shows why
ordinary-traffic disruption cannot be omitted. Manzil’s contribution is consequently not a new detector,
shortest-path algorithm, or reinforcement-learning family. It is the controlled evaluation of an integrated
and traceable prototype in which perception and simulation evidence remain distinct, normal controllers
