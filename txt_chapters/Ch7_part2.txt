7.1.5 Ambulance Routing and Rerouting
The routing graph in manzil/routing/graph.py uses SUMO edges as nodes. An arc joins two edges

only when SUMO has a lane connection between them that an emergency vehicle may use, so every
route can be driven and contains no U-turns. The edge cost follows Equation 5.4: the free-flow running
time at the currently reported speed limit, plus 2 s per halting vehicle divided by the number of lanes,
plus a declared 10 s signal delay on signalized edges, and an infinite cost for a closed edge. Snapshots
older than five steps or with negative costs are rejected. Dijkstra is the reference search. A* uses the
straight-line distance divided by the maximum speed, scaled by 0.896, the smallest ratio of edge length
CHAPTER 7. IMPLEMENTATION AND TEST CASES  95

to node distance on the grid. The scaling is needed because SUMO edge lengths exclude the junction
interiors, and it keeps the heuristic admissible.

The mission logic in manzil/routing/mission.py implements Algorithm 1. The route is refreshed
every 5 s from the ambulance’s current edge. A closure on the remaining route is accepted at once.
Otherwise a cheaper route is adopted only if it saves more than δ = 10 s for at least τ = 10 s, with a 30
s cooldown after each revision. Each accepted route becomes a new numbered version with its trigger,
and rejected candidates are logged. Static routing, used as the comparison arm, plans once on free-flow
costs at dispatch.

7.1.6 Green Corridor

The corridor coordinator in manzil/corridor/coordinator.py implements Algorithm 2 with a look-
ahead of the next two signalized junctions on the ambulance’s remaining route. A junction is requested
when it enters the look-ahead and activated when the ambulance’s free-flow arrival time is within 15
s plus the time needed to clear the queue on its approach. Activation gives the corridor ownership of
that junction in the safety filter, so normal requests are held, and the corridor requests the phase that
serves the ambulance’s movement. Priority lasts at most 60 s. Passage is detected when the ambulance
leaves the approach edge, after which ownership returns to normal control through the safety filter, and
recovery lasts until the junction’s queue is back to its mean of the 120 s before activation. A route
revision cancels requests at junctions that are no longer on the route.

A live dashboard run showed the ambulance stopping at red lights on its route, so the team added a per-
second record of why the ambulance stood still and measured it in 20 runs per background controller.
The main cause was a defect. After a route revision, SUMO keeps the edges already driven in the
vehicle’s route, so SUMO’s route index was ahead of Manzil’s route plan, and the corridor skipped the
junction directly ahead of the ambulance. The coordinator now finds the ambulance’s position by edge.
With this fix the ambulance was never first in line at a red light at a corridor junction. Its remaining stops
were vehicles queued in front of it and a permissive left turn at the final junction.

Four further changes were then tested on medium and heavy demand with five seeds and both fixed-time
and DQN background control: earlier activation with a look-ahead of three junctions, shortening the
current green for a corridor request, making ordinary vehicles give way with SUMO’s blue-light device,
and all three together. Only the shortened green was kept. In the resulting version of the corridor, a
corridor request may end the current green after 5 s instead of the normal 10 s. Yellow and all-red are
never shortened, normal control always keeps the full 10 s, and the safety monitor treats a green shorter
than 10 s as a violation unless the corridor owns the junction, and a green shorter than 5 s as a violation in

every case. Earlier activation gave small and inconsistent gains while increasing cross-street queueing
by 4 to 14 percent. The blue-light device made the ambulance cross red lights; once red lights were
enforced again, it caused gridlocks, because it needs SUMO’s sublane model. It remains an option that
is off by default. The measured effects are reported in Chapter 9.

7.1.7 Vehicle Detection, Tracking, and Counting

Perception runs as three Kaggle jobs on two Tesla T4 GPUs. The first job, kaggle/yolo_data, builds
the training data. UA-DETRAC labels four vehicle types, which are mapped to the project classes: car
and van to car, bus to bus, and others to truck. Every tenth frame is used to avoid near-duplicates, regions
that UA-DETRAC marks as ignored are filled grey, and the validation set consists of six whole training
sequences, so frames of one sequence never appear in two splits. UA-DETRAC has no motorcycle
labels, so COCO 2017 [27] images that contain a motorcycle are added, with all four classes labelled, to
stop the detector from forgetting that class. Table 7.4 gives the resulting splits.

The second job, kaggle/yolo_train, fine-tunes the COCO-pretrained YOLO11s [5] for 50 epochs at
640 pixels with batch size 32 and seed 0. Training took 1.8 h and the best validation epoch was 40. The
19 MB weights are kept in the repository with their SHA-256 checksum. The third job, kaggle/yolo_
track, runs the fine-tuned detector with ByteTrack [2] at the full 25 frames per second on all 40 test
sequences, which is 56,340 frames, at 40 to 66 frames per second.

The counting logic in manzil/perception/counting.py is shared between the Kaggle job and the
local tests. A full-width line is placed at 60 percent of the image height, and a track is counted once,
when its box centre crosses the line, with its class taken as the majority over the track. Crossings inside
ignored regions are dropped. Because UA-DETRAC gives true track identities, true counts are produced
by passing the annotated boxes through the same counter, so the count error measures detection and
tracking only. UA-DETRAC has no stop lines, so the queue indicator is a proxy: the number of tracked
vehicles that moved less than a quarter of their box height in one second. A Kaggle script job must be a
single file, so a build script copies the counting module into the job, and a test fails if that copy is out of
date.

7.1.8 Dashboard and API

The FastAPI service in manzil/api/ serves the built dashboard and the API on port 8000. One live run
executes in a worker thread with the same step order as the batch runner: snapshot, routing, corridor,
controller, safety filter, and adapter. Dashboard commands such as dispatching an ambulance or closing a
road are queued and executed between two simulation steps, so only the worker thread touches SUMO.
The live run uses the TraCI back end, so a SUMO failure cannot stop the API. The API offers run
start, stop, and speed, network geometry, current state, ambulance dispatch, and road closure, and a
WebSocket stream sends the state about ten times per second.

The dashboard in dashboard/ is written in React 19 and TypeScript and built with Vite. It draws the
network as plain SVG from the geometry in the SUMO network file. It shows the signal colour of
every link at its stop line, the vehicles, queue shading on each road, closed roads, the ambulance with its
current route version, and the corridor state of each junction. Chapter 8 explains how to use it.
